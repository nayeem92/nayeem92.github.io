The cheapest mistake in football

Coaches treat giving the ball away as bad, full stop. I checked 730,000 times it happened. In the attacking third it is safer than doing nothing at all — and players are turning down the pass that finding recommends.

3,961 matches2.4M passing options scored24 competitions

Every football coach says the same thing: don't give it away. It is the first instruction a child gets and the last one a professional hears before kick-off. It is also, depending on where you are standing, close to meaningless.

I wanted one number: when a team loses the ball at a given spot on the pitch, what does it actually cost them? Not a feeling — a number. So I took every failed open-play pass in StatsBomb's free data, around 730,000 of them across nearly 4,000 matches, and watched what happened over the next five seconds to the next minute.

01

Where you lose it changes everything

Give the ball away in your own third and the opposition really does get a decent chance at you fairly often. Give it away in their third and almost nothing happens.

And not just "less bad". Look at the third number.

Chance conceded in the 15 seconds after losing the ball, against the rate a team concedes in any 15 seconds of play.

That is the whole finding in one line. Losing the ball high up the pitch is not a smaller risk than average. It is a negative one. The ball is eighty yards from your goal and the opponent has to carry it all the way back — by the time they have, your shape has reset.

It takes about 1,220 attacking-third turnovers to concede a single expected goal.

02

So what should a player do differently?

If giving it away up there costs almost nothing, then when you pick who to pass to in the final third, what should weigh on you is how dangerous the pass is if it comes off. Whether it comes off shouldn't weigh much at all.

To test that I needed to know what each player's options actually were. StatsBomb's tracking data freezes the moment of every pass — where every teammate and opponent stood. For each visible teammate I asked two questions: how dangerous would we be if the ball got there, and how likely is it to get there. Multiply, and you can rank the options.

Players are turning down the riskier ball

In the final third, the pass at the top of that ranking lands about half the time. The pass players actually choose lands about three-quarters of the time.

Seven times out of ten in the final third, the option the model prefers is less likely to arrive than the one the player chose — and it goes further forward. So the caution is real, it is measurable, and it is concentrated exactly where the risk is cheapest.

The pass you are not playing because it might not come off is usually the right pass. Not the impossible one — the one that works half the time.

03

Does it actually cost teams anything?

A tendency is only interesting if it shows up in results. So I aggregated it: for each team in each match, how far down the available ranking did its players choose?

First question — is that even a stable thing about a team, or just noise? It is stable. In fact it is more consistent from match to match than the team's own xG, which makes sense once you say it out loud: a team plays 400 passes a game and takes a dozen shots.

Then, comparing teams of similar quality against similar opponents, with similar possession: teams whose players pick higher up the ranking create more. About 8% more open-play danger per standard deviation — roughly four expected goals across a 38-game season. It survives a placebo test and holds within the same team from match to match.

Modest, then. Not a revolution. But real, and consistent across every way I tried to slice it.

04

Then I tried to break it

The most interesting part of this project was the attempt to prove myself wrong. Three obvious objections, taken in order.

"You found that by fishing through the data"

Fair. So I rebuilt the entire system while holding back four competitions — Euro 2024, Women's Euro 2025 and two Ligue 1 seasons — wrote the prediction down first, and then looked. The effect came back the same size. On the smaller sample it is not as statistically clean, but it did not shrink, which is what a fishing artefact would have done.

"Teams protecting a lead pass safe on purpose"

This one I expected to sting. It didn't — because the premise turns out to be mostly false. Teams choose almost identically whether leading, level or trailing. The intuition is strong and the data does not support it.

"You only see what the television showed"

The real one. Every option set is built from one camera angle, and it turns out the broadcast has, on average, under a third of the pitch in shot. Three of a team's ten outfield teammates are simply missing from every decision I scored.

But this objection inverted when I measured it. On the matches where the camera showed most of the pitch — where the option sets are closest to complete — the effect is twice as strong. Bad camera coverage is hiding the finding, not inventing it. Every number here is a floor.

05

Where it is not free

One honest complication, because a coach will raise it immediately. "Cheap" here means cheap in danger conceded. It does not mean cheap in territory.

Lose the ball in the final third and a minute later the ball sits roughly forty metres further from the opponent's goal than where you lost it — the biggest loss of ground anywhere on the pitch. You don't concede. You do have to walk all the way back and start again.

What this does not show

  • It is not proof of cause. Teams that choose better create more. Whether the choosing is what causes the creating, I can't say from this.
  • It is not an argument for the Hollywood ball. The pass being recommended completes about half the time. Nothing here tested the one-in-ten defence-splitter.
  • It can't see the passes nobody tries. The cost was measured on passes players chose to attempt, and applied to options they declined. If the truly wild balls fail in messier ways, that doesn't show up here. This is the one limitation more data of this kind cannot fix.
